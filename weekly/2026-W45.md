- Nonfiction led the week with 10,610 breakouts, making it the first broad category worth investigating for narrower publishing opportunities.

- The median review count at breakout was zero, showing that a substantial existing review history was not universal among new books reaching bestseller lists.

Amazon KDP recorded 75,541 new books reaching Best Sellers lists during 2026-W45, covering October 27 through November 2. Of those, 38,398 were independently published through KDP or other indie publishing routes. The biggest single category was Nonfiction, with 10,610 breakouts. The busiest day was October 28, when 33,460 new books reached bestseller lists.

For publishers planning their next release, two figures deserve attention. The median review count at breakout was zero, and the median price was $11.99. That suggests a new book does not necessarily need an established review history to reach a bestseller list. It also gives publishers a useful price reference, although category and format still matter more than a single market-wide median.

## October 28 accounted for the week's biggest breakout surge

The daily figures show a heavily concentrated week. October 27 produced 17,751 new breakouts, followed by 33,460 on October 28. That was the week's busiest day by a wide margin. The count then fell to 5,305 on October 29 and 7,437 on October 30. The remaining days recorded 4,633 on October 31, 5,554 on November 1 and 1,401 on November 2.

The October 28 figure is nearly twice the October 27 figure. It is also several times larger than the daily totals recorded for most of the remaining period. Publishers should be careful about interpreting this as a sudden change in reader demand. A breakout count measures new books reaching bestseller lists, not the number of books purchased that day. Publication schedules, category movements and list activity can all affect the result.

The last day's lower figure also needs context. November 2 is included in the reporting period, but its 1,401 breakouts should not automatically be treated as evidence of a market slowdown. A single day's list movement is a weak basis for changing a publishing plan.

For practical planning, the useful signal is the size of the weekly opportunity rather than the precise daily pattern. There was substantial new-book activity across the period. Publishers should monitor their intended categories over several weeks before treating one unusually busy day as a repeatable launch window.

## Nonfiction leads, but several other categories offer room

Nonfiction led the category rankings with 10,610 breakouts. Literature & Fiction followed with 4,552, while Religion & Spirituality recorded 3,989. Travel reached 3,730, and Children's Books recorded 3,485. These are the five largest categories in the supplied figures.

The next group is worth watching. Computers & Technology produced 3,351 breakouts, Foreign Languages reached 3,245 and Business & Money recorded 2,876. These categories have smaller totals than Nonfiction, but that does not automatically make them easier markets. A category with fewer breakouts may also have fewer readers or less consistent demand.

For KDP publishers, **Nonfiction is the broadest starting point**, not necessarily the easiest publishing opportunity. A general nonfiction book faces a very different competitive environment from a narrowly focused guide. The same applies to Travel and Business & Money. Topic selection, search intent and the quality of competing books will determine whether a specific idea has a realistic chance.

Children's Books deserves attention from publishers considering illustrated stories, educational titles and activity books. However, the category total does not separate those formats. A children's coloring book and a children's fiction title can serve very different buyers. Publishers should investigate individual subcategories rather than treating the entire category as one market.

The practical next step is to compare actual competing titles, their BSR, prices and review counts within a narrow category. The category breakout figures show where new books are reaching bestseller lists. They do not show which keywords have the weakest competition or which subjects have the strongest conversion rates.

## Indie publishers supplied 38,398 recorded breakouts

Independently published KDP books accounted for 38,398 breakouts in the publisher classification chart. Traditional publishers accounted for 23,762, while 13,381 were unclassified. Indie publishing therefore represents a substantial part of the recorded breakout activity.

There is a data qualification worth making explicit. These three classifications add up to 65,541, not the headline total of 75,541. The difference is 10,000 books. The publisher classification figures therefore do not fully reconcile with the weekly headline figure. The indie count itself is reported as 38,398, but publishers should not use this chart as a complete breakdown of all 75,541 breakouts.

Even with that limitation, the indie figure is useful. It confirms that independently published books are reaching Amazon Best Sellers lists across the reporting period. This is encouraging for publishers who do not have access to a traditional publishing distribution network. It is not proof that every KDP niche offers attractive returns.

There is another distinction between reaching a list and building a sustainable publishing business. A breakout is a new book on a New Releases list within the last 60 days that reached a Best Sellers list. It does not establish how long the book stayed there, how many sales followed or whether the author recovered production and advertising costs.

For a publisher deciding what to release next, the sensible response is to look for repeatable demand. Check whether multiple indie titles in the same subcategory are achieving competitive BSRs. Then examine their prices, review histories and product formats. One successful launch is interesting. A group of competing titles with consistent activity is a more useful market signal.

## The $11.99 median price gives publishers a reference point

The weekly median book price was $11.99. The price distribution shows that cheaper books remained active, but the higher price brackets also contributed substantial breakout numbers. Books priced below $5 accounted for 17,701 breakouts. The $5–9.99 range recorded 17,301, while $10–14.99 produced 13,823.

The $15–19.99 bracket recorded 10,869 breakouts. Books priced at $20 or more accounted for 15,044. The distribution does not show a simple relationship between lower prices and more bestseller appearances. In fact, the $20+ group produced more breakouts than either of the two middle price brackets.

This matters for publishers of coloring books, cookbooks and illustrated children's books. Production costs and perceived value can justify a higher price, particularly when a book includes substantial artwork, useful reference material or a distinctive format. A low price is not automatically the right strategy.

The $11.99 median is a useful starting point for checking comparable books. It is not a recommended price for every format. A short low-content journal, a full-color children's book and a substantial cookbook have different printing costs and buyer expectations. Compare like with like before setting a list price.

There is also no guarantee that a book priced in a popular bracket will achieve similar results. The chart counts breakouts, not profit. A publisher needs to account for printing costs, royalties, advertising and the number of sales required to recover the investment.

## What KDP publishers should investigate next week

The most useful finding from 2026-W45 is the combination of substantial indie activity and a median breakout review count of zero. A new title does not necessarily need a large review history to reach a bestseller list. That is encouraging, but it should not be mistaken for evidence that reviews are irrelevant. The figure describes the median among breakouts, not the review requirements of every competitive category.

For low-content publishers, the next research step is to examine narrow journal, planner and activity-book categories. Look for competing books with recent publication dates and meaningful BSR movement. A category with regular new entrants may offer opportunities, but it can also indicate strong competition. The weekly figures do not separate low-content books from other formats, so category-level investigation is necessary.

Coloring-book publishers should pay particular attention to Children's Books and related subcategories. Check whether successful titles target a clear age group or subject rather than relying on broad coloring keywords. Cookbook publishers can use Nonfiction as a starting point, then compare specific food interests, dietary topics and practical cooking formats. These are research directions, not claims that any particular keyword is currently profitable.

Fiction publishers face a different problem. The supplied examples show the strength of established authors and series. A new author cannot assume that a high-profile release's BSR will translate into comparable demand for an unknown title. A more useful comparison is between recent books in the same subgenre, especially those with similar prices and review histories.

Finally, keep the measurement limits in view. BSR is Amazon Best Sellers Rank, where a lower number is better. Estimated units and revenue are modelled from BSR, not reported by Amazon. The breakout definition measures entry onto a Best Sellers list, not long-term commercial success. KDPTop's opportunity score can help compare category room, but this week's supplied figures do not include category-level opportunity scores. The sensible publishing decision is to combine these weekly signals with direct competitor research before committing to a manuscript or production budget.
